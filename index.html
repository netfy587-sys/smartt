A GUIDE TO THE GAME

SKILL ON GRASS AND TURF

Eleven players a side, a hard plastic ball, and sticks that never leave the ground above knee height. Field hockey is one of the most widely played team sports on earth — here's how it works, where it began, and what separates a good pass from a great one.

WHAT IS FIELD HOCKEY

A game of touch, not power

Field hockey is played on a rectangular pitch, traditionally grass but almost always artificial turf at the competitive level, with two teams of eleven trying to hit, push, or flick a ball into the opposing goal using a curved wooden or composite stick. Unlike ice hockey, there's no lifting the stick above the shoulder and no raising it dangerously near another player — the game rewards close control and quick feet over sheer force.

It's also one of the most international team sports played today, with strong domestic leagues and national programs across Europe, Asia, Australia, and Africa, and a place at every Summer Olympics since 1908.

New Zealand and Australia compete at the 2012 Summer Olympics.
WHERE THE GAME BEGAN

Origins of the sport

Stick-and-ball games go back thousands of years across several continents, but the version played today took shape in 19th-century England before spreading through the British Empire and beyond.

1849

Clubs begin to form

Some of the earliest organised hockey clubs were founded in England, adapting older folk games into a sport with agreed rules and a fixed pitch.

1901

Women's hockey takes hold

The All England Women's Hockey Association formed in 1895, and by the early 1900s the women's game was thriving in schools and universities across Britain and the United States.

1908

Onto the Olympic stage

Men's field hockey debuted at the 1908 London Olympics; the women's tournament followed in 1980, and both have been fixtures of the Summer Games since.

THE PLAYING FIELD

The pitch and the gear

Nearly every top-level match today is played on artificial turf rather than grass — it gives a truer, faster bounce and lets teams play a quicker passing game. Goals can only be scored from inside the shooting circle marked in front of each net, which shapes almost everything about how attacking play is built.

The stick

J-shaped and roughly a metre long, with only the flat side legal for playing the ball — the rounded back can't be used to strike or push it.

The ball

A hard plastic sphere about the size of a baseball, built to skim fast and true across turf without much bounce.

The turf

Water-based artificial pitches are standard in top competition, watered before matches to keep the surface fast and consistent.

Kit & shin guards

Outfield players wear light kit and mandatory shin guards and mouthguards; only the goalkeeper wears heavy, padded protective gear.

HOW A MATCH WORKS

The essential rules

Field hockey has a reputation for being complex to follow, but most of it comes down to a handful of core ideas.

01

Four quarters

Matches run 60–70 minutes, split into four quarters with short breaks between each — a format adopted to give players brief recovery windows in a fast, high-intensity game.

02

Scoring from the circle

A goal only counts if the ball is played from inside the shooting circle in front of the goal — a shot struck from outside it doesn't count, even if it goes in.

03

The flat-side rule

Only the flat face of the stick can touch the ball. Playing it with the rounded back, or raising the stick dangerously near another player, is a foul.

04

No offside

Field hockey abolished its offside rule in the early 1990s, which opened the game up and made fast breaks and long passes far more common.

05

Penalty corners

Awarded for fouls inside the circle, these set plays let the attacking team send in a fast, rehearsed shot with defenders restricted to fixed starting positions.

WHO PLAYS WHERE

Positions on the pitch

Formations vary by team and era, but most sides still organise around four broad roles.

Goalkeeper

The only player allowed to use their body to stop the ball inside the circle, wearing full protective padding built specifically for the position.

Defenders

Positioned to break up attacks and start play from the back, using tackles and interceptions to win back possession cleanly.

Midfielders

The link between defence and attack, covering the most distance of any player and shaping the tempo of the game.

Forwards

Focused on creating and finishing chances inside the circle, often working in pairs or trios to stretch a defence.

BUILDING THE FUNDAMENTALS

Core skills players develop

The best field hockey players tend to master the same small set of techniques, then combine them at speed.

Dribbling

Keeping the ball close with quick, light touches on the flat side of the stick while still moving at pace.

The push pass

A low, accurate pass rolled along the turf, more common than a hard hit for short and medium-range distribution.

Trapping

Cushioning an incoming pass so it stays under control at the receiver's feet rather than bouncing away.

Tackling

Timing a stick tackle to win the ball cleanly without making contact with an opponent's stick or body.

The drag flick

A specialised penalty-corner technique that whips the ball off the stick with power and disguise, often used to finish set plays.

Positional awareness

Understanding when to hold shape and when to break lines, since field hockey has no offside rule to limit attacking runs.

THE BIGGEST STAGES

Major competitions

Olympics

Field hockey has been part of the Summer Games since 1908 for men and 1980 for women, and remains the sport's most prestigious international event.

FIH Pro League

An annual home-and-away league contested by the world's top national teams, running across the year rather than in a single tournament window.

World Cup

Held every four years and organised by the International Hockey Federation, bringing together the strongest national teams from every continent.

Domestic & club leagues

Strong club competitions exist across the Netherlands, Germany, India, Australia, and beyond, feeding talent into national programs.
